\begin{abstract}
Demonstrations are an effective way to accelerate single-agent reinforcement learning (RL), however, extending this idea to multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) is non-trivial: a lone human cannot realistically provide demonstrations for multiple agents simultaneously. Recent work on Round-Robin Behavior Cloning (R2BC) provides a solution by allowing a demonstrator to control a single agent at a time while the others execute their own current policies. In this work, we propose \textit{Imitation Bootstrapped Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (IBMARL)}, a framework which extends IBRL to cooperative teams: at each time step the imitation and RL policies each propose a joint action, and an ensemble of centralized critics scores the two and
samples between them. We evaluate IBMARL on two sparse-reward simulation tasks where it significantly outperforms baselines methods in sample efficiency.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
multi-agent reinforcement learning, imitation learning, learning from demonstration, multi-robot systems
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Cooperative multi-robot tasks are difficult for RL to solve: the joint-action space grows exponentially with the number of agents, credit assignment across agents is ambiguous, and rewards are often inherently sparse \cite{albrecht2024marl}. In single-agent RL, demonstrations reliably overcome similar obstacles---methods such as DQfD \cite{hester2018dqfd}, DDPGfD \cite{vecerik2017ddpgfd}, and DDPG + BC \cite{nair2018overcoming} seed the replay buffer or regularize the learner toward expert data, and Imitation Bootstrapped RL (IBRL) \cite{hu2023ibrl} goes further by keeping a behavior-cloned policy in the loop, using it to propose actions during exploration and to improve the bootstrap target. \\
The obstacle to extending such frameworks to MARL is practicality rather than algorithm design: nearly all multi-agent imitation learning (MAIL) assumes joint demonstrations, in which an expert must simultaneously command all $N$ agents---data a lone human operator cannot realistically provide for a physically coupled team \cite{mattson2026r2bc, turco2024cognitive}. Round-Robin Behavior Cloning (R2BC) \cite{mattson2026r2bc} resolves this by having the demonstrator cycle through each team member one at a time while the others execute their current learned policies, yielding decentralized cooperative policies from realistic single-agent demonstrations. However, R2BC inherits the classic limitations of behavior cloning: covariate shift, a performance ceiling at the demonstrator, and a prohibitive demonstration burden for the last increments of task performance. \\
\indent This work combines MARL and MAIL. Assuming access to a sparse ground-truth team reward, we ask: \emph{Can policies trained from realistic single-agent demonstrations bootstrap MARL?} Our contributions are:
%I think it makes sense that IL bootstraps RL not the other way around. As IL helps start RL not the other way around.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate \textbf{IBMARL}, a generalization of IBRL to cooperative Dec-POMDPs in which the imitation learned (IL) policy is a set of $N$ decentralized R2BC policies rather than a single centralized policy.
    \item On 2 difficult sparse-reward tasks in VMAS \cite{bettini2022vmas}, IBMARL significantly outperforms baselines in terms of sample efficiency. Specifically, on the physically coupled Buzzwire task, IBMARL surpasses its R2BC initialization $1.8\times$ faster than both a MARL-from-demonstrations baseline and an RL fine-tuning baseline.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Related work.} In single-agent RL, demonstrations pretrain the policy \cite{rajeswaran2018dapg}, prefill the replay buffer \cite{vecerik2017ddpgfd, hester2018dqfd}, or regularize the objective \cite{nair2018overcoming}, and IBRL takes the higher-valued of the IL and RL actions at both action-selection and bootstrapping time \cite{hu2023ibrl}. MARL extensions remain limited \cite{albrecht2024marl}: prior work lets an expert control a single agent during environment interaction \cite{alagha2023democloning} or distills coordination from a centralized IL policy into decentralized learners \cite{sartoretti2019primal}. Closest to our setting is PegMARL \cite{yu2025pegmarl}, which combines single-agent demonstrations with sparse-reward MARL via discriminator-based reward shaping; its demonstrations are, however, provided with no other agents present, leaving all of the teaching of cooperation to the environment reward. IBMARL differs in that its imitation prior is trained from R2BC demonstrations collected online \emph{in the presence of} teammates, and the IL policy acts through action proposals and bootstrap targets rather than reward shaping. To our knowledge, no prior work extends IBRL-style bootstrapping to the multi-agent setting.

\section{Problem Formulation and Background}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Cooperative Dec-POMDPs}
Following the notation of R2BC~\cite{mattson2026r2bc}, we formulate
cooperative multi-robot learning as a decentralized partially observable
Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP)~\cite{oliehoek2016decpomdp} with
agents $\mathcal{I} = \{1,\dots,N\}$, joint action space
$\mathcal{A} = \mathcal{A}_1 \times \dots \times \mathcal{A}_N$, joint
observation space $\Omega = \Omega_1 \times \dots \times \Omega_N$, and
discount factor $\gamma$. We consider \emph{shared-reward} games with a
fixed horizon $T$, in which agent $i$ receives a local observation
$o_{i,t} \in \Omega_i$ and selects an individual action
$a_{i,t} \in \mathcal{A}_i$. Distinct from standard MARL, we utilize two
products of a R2BC training session: (i)~a \emph{frozen} decentralized
imitation policy $\pi^{\il} = (\pi^{\il}_1, \dots, \pi^{\il}_N)$,
$\pi^{\il}_i : \Omega_i \rightarrow \mathcal{A}_i$, which we call the
\emph{IL policy}, and (ii)~its demonstration set
$\mathcal{D} = \{\mathcal{D}_1, \dots, \mathcal{D}_N\}$ of per-agent
observation--action transitions. The objective is to learn RL policies
$\pi_{\theta} = (\pi_{\theta_1}, \dots, \pi_{\theta_N})$ that surpass the
IL policy with as little online environment interaction as possible.

\subsection{R2BC}
R2BC~\cite{mattson2026r2bc} trains decentralized per-agent BC policies
from a demonstrator who cycles round-robin through the team, controlling
one agent in its own action space while the rest execute their current
policies; because demonstrations are never given in the joint action
space, a lone human can teach the whole team online. 
\subsection{IBRL}
\indent IBRL~\cite{hu2023ibrl} keeps a pretrained IL policy frozen during
off-policy RL and uses it in two places: at interaction time the agent
executes whichever of the IL and RL proposals has the higher value under
the target Q-function,
$a_t = \arg\max_{a \in \{a^{\il}, a^{\rl}\}} Q_{\phi'}(s, a)$, and the
temporal-difference target maximizes over the same pair at the next
state. Even with few demonstrations and poor standalone BC, this
combination achieves strong real-robot performance~\cite{hu2023ibrl}.

\subsection{Tasks}
We instantiate the problem on the \textit{Navigation} and \textit{Buzzwire} tasks of the
Vectorized Multi-Agent Simulator (VMAS)~\cite{bettini2022vmas} (Fig.~\ref{fig:tasks}).
In \textit{Navigation}, $N{=}3$ agents and their assigned goals spawn at random in a bounded 2-D workspace, each agent observing its own state, its goal's relative position, and a LiDAR scan of its teammates.
In \textit{Buzzwire}, $N{=}2$ agents are connected to a ball and must thread it through a narrow corridor to a goal without touching the walls; each agent observes its own position, velocity and relative position to the goal location.
In both scenarios, agents act through continuous velocity commands over a fixed horizon $T$.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\caption{The two VMAS tasks. (a) Navigation: three agents (LiDAR rays shown) each reach their color-matched goal; adapted from~\cite{mattson2026r2bc}. (b) Buzzwire: two agents joined to a ball by rigid links carry it along the corridor to the goal (green).}
\label{fig:tasks}

\indent The native VMAS rewards are dense, which masks the exploration problem
that demonstrations are meant to solve. We therefore replace them with a
sparse variant in which an agent is rewarded only once it is near its
goal in \textit{Navigation}, and only once the ball is near the goal in
\textit{Buzzwire}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_{i,t} \;=\;
\begin{cases}
\tilde{r}_{i,t} & \text{if } d_{i,t} < \delta,\\[2pt]
-1 & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $d_{i,t}$ is agent $i$'s distance to its goal (Navigation) or the
ball's distance to the goal, shared by both agents (Buzzwire), and
$\tilde{r}_{i,t}$ is the small native VMAS shaping reward. Episodes
always run the full horizon, so an agent's undiscounted return is
approximately the negative number of steps spent outside the goal region,
bounded below by $-T$. Navigation uses $T = 100$ and $\delta = 0.4$; Buzzwire uses
$T = 200$ and $\delta = 0.1$, below the corridor half-width. The radius
$\delta$ sets the sparsity, and hence the difficulty, of the task, so the
difficulty can be set deliberately to measure the effect of IBMARL.

\section{Imitation Bootstrapped MARL}
\label{sec:method}
IBMARL extends IBRL~\cite{hu2023ibrl} to the cooperative multi-agent
setting under centralized training with decentralized
execution~\cite{lowe2017maddpg}. The IL policy participates in training
through three mechanisms: it \emph{proposes actions} during exploration,
it \emph{proposes bootstrap actions} for the temporal-difference target,
and its demonstrations \emph{pre-populate} the replay buffer. The choice
between IL and RL proposals is made not by a fixed schedule but by a
critic-scored \emph{arbiter}, described next, so the IL policy is followed
exactly as long as the critics judge it to be the better option.

\subsection{Proposals and the Arbiter}
Given a team observation $\obs = (o_1, \dots, o_N)$, each agent forms two
candidate actions,
\begin{equation}
a^{\rl}_i = \pi_{\theta_i}(o_i) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad
a^{\il}_i = \pi^{\il}_i(o_i) + \varepsilon'_i,
\end{equation}
with i.i.d.\ exploration noise
$\varepsilon, \varepsilon' \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ applied to both
proposals so that arbitration compares like with like, assembled into two
joint candidates $\act^{\il} = (a^{\il}_1, \dots, a^{\il}_N)$ and
$\act^{\rl} = (a^{\rl}_1, \dots, a^{\rl}_N)$. Keeping the
choice between the two policies preserves whatever coordination each
policy encodes and makes the candidate set size independent of team size
$N$; a variant that mixes the two policies within each proposal is ablated
in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}. Each agent maintains an ensemble of $E{=}3$
centralized critics $Q^{k}_i(\obs, \act)$ with target copies
$\bar{Q}^{k}_i$. Following REDQ~\cite{chen2021redq}, as adopted by IBRL,
the arbiter samples two target members $K$, $\lvert K \rvert = 2$, and
scores each candidate $c\in\{\il,\rl\}$ by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score}
\bar{Q}(c) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \, \min_{k \in K} \,
\bar{Q}^{k}_i\!\left(\obs, \act^{c}\right).
\end{equation}
The executed candidate is sampled from a Boltzmann distribution over the
two scores, $c^\ast \sim \operatorname{softmax}_c\big(\bar{Q}(c)/\beta\big)$
with temperature $\beta = 0.05$ (\emph{soft} arbitration), which retains a
small probability of executing the lower-valued candidate and so keeps
supplying the critics with transitions from both policies; taking the
argmax instead is the \emph{hard} variant.

\subsection{Learning}
Critics are trained with a TD(0) target that bootstraps through the
arbiter: at the next observation $\obs'$, the arbiter is applied to
noise-free proposals from the target actors $\bar{\pi}_{\theta}$ and the
IL policy, yielding a bootstrap action $\act'^{\ast}$, and every ensemble
member regresses to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:target}
y_i = r_i + \gamma\,(1 - \mathds{1}{\text{[terminal]}})\,
\min_{k \in K} \bar{Q}^{k}_i(\obs', \act'^{\ast}),
\end{equation}
so members differ only by initialization, which is what makes the
minimum over a random pair in \eqref{eq:score} a pessimistic estimate
rather than noise. Actors are updated with the deterministic policy
gradient of MADDPG~\cite{lowe2017maddpg,lillicrap2016ddpg} against a fixed
single member, $\mathcal{L}(\theta_i) = -\,\mathbb{E}\big[Q^{1}_i(\obs,
\pi_{\theta}(\obs))\big]$, preserving parity with the single-critic
baselines; actor and critic targets are updated by Polyak averaging with
rate $\tau$.

\subsection{Data and Implementation}
IBMARL keeps a single replay buffer per team member, pre-loaded with all
demonstration transitions in $\mathcal{D}$ and sampled uniformly, in the
style of IBRL: online transitions are appended and gradually dilute the
demonstrations, with no separate demonstration buffer and no mixing
schedule. Updates begin once the buffer holds $W$ online steps; during
this warm-up the IL policy's noised proposal is executed unconditionally,
as in IBRL's warm-up episodes, so that the first arbitrated decisions are
made by critics that have already observed the IL policy reach the goal.
Actors are
decentralized per-agent MLPs (two hidden layers of 256 units, $\tanh$);
critics are centralized over the team's observations and actions.
Training alternates collection of $F$ environment steps with $G$ gradient
steps at batch size 1024 (Adam, learning rate $10^{-4}$, $\gamma = 0.99$,
$\tau = 10^{-3}$, $\sigma = 0.1$, gradient-norm clipping at $1.0$), with
$F/G/W = 1000/500/5{\times}10^{3}$ on Navigation and
$8000/2000/4{\times}10^{4}$ on Buzzwire, replay capacities of
$2{\times}10^{5}$ and $4{\times}10^{5}$ transitions, and budgets of
$3{\times}10^{5}$ and $6{\times}10^{5}$ environment steps, so the ratio of
gradient steps to environment steps is the same on both tasks. The implementation is built on
TorchRL~\cite{bou2024torchrl}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{IL policies and demonstrations.} On each task the IL policy is
the decentralized policy of one R2BC training session (one MLP per agent with
a single hidden layer of 8 ReLU units), trained from demonstrations given through R2BC's round-robin protocol by
a synthetic demonstrator: a MAPPO policy trained with the dense reward on
Navigation, and a scripted controller that holds each agent at a fixed
lateral offset while driving toward the goal on Buzzwire. The budget is intentionally small: 24 episodes on Navigation and 12 on Buzzwire, 2{,}400 transitions each.
Twelve was chosen on Buzzwire because the IL policy's return rises
sharply beyond it ($-90$ at 14 against $-133$ at 12); this IL policy
reaches the goal in 88\% of episodes but does not remain there, leaving a
clear margin for RL. The frozen IL policies attain $-48.7$ and $-137.5$.
All
demonstration-based methods consume the same transitions and, where
applicable, the same IL policy checkpoint.

\textbf{Baselines} share IBMARL's architectures, DDPG-style
updates~\cite{lillicrap2016ddpg}, exploration noise and hyperparameters,
with a single critic per agent, so that \emph{how} demonstrations are used
is the only variable. \textbf{MADDPG}~\cite{lowe2017maddpg} is the
demonstration-free reference. \textbf{RLfD}~\cite{vecerik2017ddpgfd}
keeps the demonstrations in a separate replay buffer from which each
minibatch draws a fraction annealed linearly from 50\% to 0\% over
training. \textbf{RFT} adds a behavior-cloning regularizer
$\alpha\,\lambda(t)\,\lVert \pi_{\theta}(o^{\mathcal{D}}) -
a^{\mathcal{D}} \rVert_2^2$ to the actor
loss~\cite{rajeswaran2018dapg,nair2018overcoming}, with $\alpha = 0.1$
and $\lambda(t)$ annealed from 1 to 0 over the first half of training.

\textbf{Ablations.} \emph{Per-agent mixing} expands the candidate set to
all $2^N$ per-agent IL/RL combinations; \emph{hard} replaces
Boltzmann sampling with argmax; \emph{single-critic} sets $E{=}1$.

\textbf{Protocol.} Policies are evaluated without exploration noise on a
dedicated environment, 20 episodes every $10^3$ steps on Navigation and
40 episodes every $3.2{\times}10^4$ steps on Buzzwire. IBMARL is
evaluated with the policy it executes, in which the target critics
arbitrate greedily between the IL policy's and the RL actors' joint actions,
as IBRL reports. We report the per-agent mean return, smoothed with a
centered window of 9 evaluations on Navigation and 3 on Buzzwire; bands
show $\pm 1$ s.e.m.

\textbf{Buzzwire.} This task is considerably harder: MADDPG never
reaches the goal in any seed ($-194.6 \pm 5.7$ against a floor of $-200$).

$1.8\times$ fewer than RLfD and RFT (296k each), and at 200k steps its
return is $-108.5$ against $-170.2$ for RLfD and $-156.8$ for RFT; its
final return, $-84.5 \pm 14.8$, is comparable to RLfD's $-85.9 \pm 18.2$. At the end of training the arbiter still selects the IL policy's joint
action in 29--50\% of evaluation decisions. The arbiter variants are indistinguishable
(Table~\ref{tab:results}): \emph{per-agent mixing} crosses the
IL policy at a median of 136k steps and \emph{hard} and
\emph{single-critic} at 168k, with final returns within seed noise.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
Our results have shown that by combining MARL and R2BC we were able to
produce a sample-efficient method that reaches near optimal performance
sooner than either could achieve individually. By anchoring both
exploration and the bootstrap target to IL proposals, IBMARL replaces
MARL's joint-action search with a choice between two coherent joint
actions, which is most valuable when moderate demonstration data reaches
much of the task but closing the remaining gap with demonstrations alone
would demand far more human effort than a small amount of RL fine-tuning;
once online interaction is plentiful, replay-based RLfD catches up, so
IBMARL's advantage is best characterized as sample efficiency, the binding
constraint for physical multi-robot teams. The IL warm-up matters most on
Buzzwire, where RL alone never reaches the goal: without it, an untrained
critic's first arbitrations are arbitrary, and a run whose opening batches are RL-heavy may never observe a success. Per-agent mixing slowing
learning on Navigation is counterintuitive, as it strictly enlarges the
candidate set, but a mixed candidate is one that neither policy would
produce, so its value is extrapolated by critics that have not seen such
transitions. The penalty does not appear on Buzzwire, where the two variants are
indistinguishable. Like R2BC and IBRL, we implicitly assume near-optimal demonstrations;
suboptimal demonstrations can misdirect value prediction, and filtering
to transitions where the critic believes the demonstrator is better than
the learner~\cite{nair2018overcoming} is an unevaluated safeguard. More
simulated tasks and hardware validation are the natural next steps.

\section{Conclusion}
We introduced IBMARL, in which a decentralized R2BC IL policy proposes
actions during exploration, shapes bootstrap targets, and pre-populates
the replay buffer of centralized-critic MARL. IBMARL surpasses its IL policy
with $2.8$--$4.6\times$ fewer environment interactions than
demonstration-based baselines on Navigation and $1.8\times$ fewer than
both on Buzzwire, suggesting that realistic single-agent demonstrations are a practical
lever for sample-efficient sparse-reward MARL on physical multi-robot
teams.
